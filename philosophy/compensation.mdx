Compensation breaks trust slowly and quietly — and then all at once. An employee who discovers a peer at the same level earns significantly more doesn't just feel underpaid. They feel deceived. A manager who can't explain why one offer was approved and another wasn't learns not to advocate. A leadership team that can't defend its comp decisions to the board, or to itself, starts making exceptions that compound into inequity. The antidote to all of this is a comp philosophy built from first principles: one that is defensible, consistent, and motivating — in that order.

My approach to compensation isn't about picking a percentile and calling it a philosophy. It's about building the architecture — the job levels, the survey methodology, the equity framework, the governance — so that every individual pay decision sits inside a structure that can be explained and defended. When someone asks "why is my salary X?", the answer should never be "that's just how it came out." It should be: here's the role level, here's the market data, here's where you sit in the range, and here's the path to moving.

## Job Architecture and Leveling

Job architecture is the skeleton of any comp program. Without clear levels, benchmarking is arbitrary, calibration is political, and promotion decisions are inconsistent. I build job architectures from scratch when needed and audit existing ones for level inflation, title sprawl, and misalignment between comp and scope.

My leveling frameworks distinguish between **individual contributor tracks** and **management tracks**, with explicit breakpoints at the transitions that matter most in fast-growing companies: the IC-to-senior transition (where scope expands from execution to ownership), the senior-to-staff transition (where scope expands from team to cross-functional), and the IC-to-manager transition (which is a career change, not a promotion).

Each level is defined by scope, autonomy, impact, and knowledge — not years of experience. That's the key design choice. Experience-based leveling creates a bias toward tenure and away from performance. Scope-based leveling keeps the conversation honest.

## Comp Program Areas

<Accordion title="Base Salary Structure">
  Base salary bands are built from market data, not from what we can afford or what someone negotiated. I anchor bands to a target percentile — typically P50 or P65 depending on the company's philosophy and competitive context — and build ranges wide enough to accommodate performance differentiation, but not so wide they become meaningless.

  At companies like OpenAI and Tools for Humanity, I've worked in highly competitive talent markets where P50 isn't competitive for certain roles. The solution isn't to set a global P75 philosophy — it's to have a principled approach to market-premium roles, documented explicitly, so it doesn't become a negotiation leverage point for individuals.

  At Mintlify's stage, I'd expect to build or significantly refine the band structure: define the levels, run the benchmarks, set the bands, and then audit every current employee against them. Pay equity remediation before growth is dramatically cheaper — in money and trust — than after.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Equity Philosophy">
  Equity is where most early-stage comp programs break down. Initial grants are set at hire based on negotiation leverage, early employees get massive grants that create internal inequity over time, and refresh programs are either nonexistent or ad hoc.

  My equity philosophy starts with the premise that equity should be a retention and alignment tool — not a hire-close mechanism. That means:

  - **Refresh grants on a consistent cadence** (typically annual), not only when someone threatens to leave
  - **Refresh sizing based on unvested value and continued scope**, not on negotiating ability
  - **Cohort equity review** — regularly examining whether early hires have meaningful unvested equity relative to later hires, and whether the distribution reflects current contribution

  At OpenAI, I reimagined incentive design from first principles in partnership with the exec team — rethinking how grants were sized, when they were issued, and what they were meant to accomplish. At Tools for Humanity, I rebuilt performance-linked comp to emphasize evidence-based feedback and reduce the variance that had accumulated through inconsistent grant sizing.

  Equity comp letters, refresh award communications, and grant processing workflows are also places I've built automation — using Claude Code and custom API tooling to handle distribution at scale without sacrificing accuracy or personalization.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Geographic Localization">
  Geographic pay policy is a genuine philosophical question, not just a technical one. Pure location-based pay (pay what the local market pays) maximizes cost efficiency but creates visible pay disparities that feel unfair to employees doing identical work. Pure role-based pay (pay everyone the same regardless of location) is simple and feels fair but overcompensates some markets and is uncompetitive in others.

  My approach is to be explicit about the philosophy before building the mechanics. Most companies land somewhere in the middle: a zone-based approach with a limited number of geographic tiers, where the tiers are defensible (major high-cost metros versus the rest of the country, or a small number of international zones), and where the policy is transparent to candidates and employees.

  The worst outcome is an implicit policy — where location affects pay but the company hasn't decided how or why, and different employees end up in different tiers based on who they negotiated with. Transparency and consistency here are non-negotiable.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Benchmarking Methodology">
  Survey selection and job matching are where benchmarking either becomes a genuine tool or theater. I've worked with Radford (now AON), Mercer, and Croner surveys in depth — both as a consumer at Google and as the designer of benchmarking programs at OpenAI and Tools for Humanity.

  The methodology I use:

  1. **Select surveys based on the talent pool, not inertia.** For tech roles, Radford is the standard. For finance or legal, Mercer is often more relevant. For early-stage companies, there are specialty surveys (Radford's startup cut, Levels.fyi cross-referenced with survey data) that better reflect actual competition.

  2. **Match jobs to survey benchmarks by scope, not title.** A "Senior Engineer" at a 65-person startup does different things than a "Senior Engineer" at Google. Match to the closest functional description, document the rationale, and flag roles where no survey match is clean.

  3. **Weight multiple data points.** I use a weighted blend of survey sources rather than anchoring to a single data point. This reduces the noise from any one survey's methodology or sample.

  4. **Refresh benchmarks annually at minimum.** In fast-moving talent markets, two-year-old benchmarks are a liability. The AI space in particular has seen significant market movement — stale data creates real comp risk.

  At Google, I built custom benchmarking approaches for Alphabet's pre-IPO subsidiaries — companies with novel job architectures where no off-the-shelf benchmark existed. That required blending survey data, external market research, and first-principles role scoping. It's a skill I've continued to use for every unusual or hybrid role I've encountered since.
</Accordion>

## Pay Equity Analysis

Pay equity work is both a compliance practice and a values practice. The mechanics are straightforward: regression analysis controlling for legitimate pay factors (level, tenure at level, geography, performance rating) to identify unexplained variance by gender, race, or other protected characteristics. The harder work is deciding what to do with what you find.

At Tools for Humanity, I rebuilt the performance and compensation program specifically to reduce pay variance that had accumulated through inconsistent processes. The intervention had three parts: standardized leveling (so we were comparing people at the same scope), calibrated performance ratings (so ratings reflected evidence, not advocacy), and a pay equity audit that identified and remediated statistically significant gaps.

The key insight is that pay equity remediation is not the same as making everyone's salary equal. It's about making sure that differences in pay are explained by legitimate, documented factors — and that those factors themselves are fair. A comp program with disciplined job architecture and evidence-based performance management is naturally more equitable, because the inputs are cleaner.

## Connecting Comp to Performance

Compensation and performance are deeply interdependent, and the connection has to be explicit and consistent. When merit budgets are distributed, the process should start from the band structure — understanding where each person sits relative to market — and then apply performance differentiation on top of that. People at the bottom of the range get more budget than people at the top, all else equal. People with strong performance ratings get more than people with developing ratings.

The failure mode I see most often is merit budgets distributed as flat percentages, disconnected from both market position and performance differentiation. The result is that high performers at the top of the range get the same increase as average performers at the bottom — and everyone is vaguely dissatisfied. Building the mechanics to connect band position, performance rating, and budget into a defensible increase matrix is one of the highest-leverage things a comp program can do.
