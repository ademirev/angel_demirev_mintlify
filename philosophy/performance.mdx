Performance management done wrong is one of the most corrosive things a company can do to its culture. It creates anxiety without clarity. It generates paperwork that no one reads. It produces ratings that feel arbitrary, calibration sessions that reward advocates over evidence, and merit increases that don't connect to anything meaningful. People learn that the game is about impression management, not actual contribution — and the best people, who have the most options, are often the quickest to draw that conclusion.

Done right, performance management does something quite different. It creates clarity: here's what excellent looks like at this level, here's where you are, here's the specific feedback that will help you grow. It creates trust: the process is fair, the calibration is evidence-based, and the outcomes are defensible. And it creates alignment: the company's priorities are reflected in what gets recognized and rewarded, so people understand what the org actually values, not just what it says it values.

My approach to performance is calibration-first, evidence-based, and manager-enabled. The mechanics follow from those principles.

## Core Design Principles

**Calibration-first.** The calibration session — where managers align on ratings across the team — is the most important part of the cycle, and it should be treated that way. Most companies run calibration as a checkbox at the end: everyone has already decided on ratings, calibration is where you defend them. I design cycles where calibration happens before ratings are finalized, so the session is genuinely evaluative rather than adversarial. The output of calibration should be ratings that are consistent across managers, not just ratings that survived a debate.

**Evidence-based.** Performance ratings should be grounded in documented evidence: specific examples of impact, feedback from collaborators, outcomes against goals. The enemy of evidence is recency bias (the last two months crowd out the full year) and narrative bias (a compelling story replaces concrete examples). I build cycles with periodic check-ins and structured self-assessments precisely to create an evidence base before ratings season, so calibration sessions aren't starting from scratch.

**Manager-enabled.** The manager is the primary delivery mechanism for performance management. If managers aren't equipped — with training, tools, clear expectations, and support from HR — the cycle fails regardless of how well-designed the process is. I invest heavily in manager preparation: calibration prep sessions, coaching on difficult conversations, templates for written feedback, and explicit guidance on what a "strong" rating actually requires.

## How to Run Calibration Well

A calibration session that works has three preconditions: a shared rubric, prepared managers, and a skilled facilitator. When those are in place, calibration becomes a genuinely productive conversation about people and their contributions — not a political negotiation.

**The shared rubric** is the level expectations document. Before any calibration session, every manager should know exactly what "meets expectations" means at each level, what "exceeds" requires, and where the bar is for the top rating. Without a shared rubric, calibration devolves into each manager arguing from their own implicit standard.

**Prepared managers** come to calibration having reviewed all the evidence — self-assessments, peer feedback, goal completion, impact examples — and having a preliminary rating for each of their reports. They can articulate the evidence behind that rating. They've thought about which cases are clear and which ones they're genuinely uncertain about. Preparation turns calibration from a discussion of impressions into a discussion of evidence.

**The facilitation role** is where HR adds significant value. A skilled calibrator does several things: surfaces inconsistencies across managers ("you've rated two people at the same level very differently — walk us through the distinction"), challenges recency bias ("the Q4 sprint was impressive, but how does the full year look?"), flags advocacy dynamics ("I want to make sure we're hearing from all managers equally before we close on this"), and keeps the room moving without cutting off important deliberation.

Common failure modes I watch for in calibration:
- **The squeaky wheel effect** — the manager who advocates most loudly wins, rather than the one with the strongest evidence
- **Compression bias** — everyone ends up in the middle bucket because no one wants to have a hard conversation
- **Recency domination** — Q4 performance crowds out everything that happened before it
- **Level inconsistency** — identical behavior is rated "exceeds" for one team and "meets" for another because the rubric isn't being applied uniformly

## Manager Coaching and Development

First-time managers and experienced managers need different things from performance support.

**First-time managers** need structure. They often don't know how to write developmental feedback that's specific and actionable. They haven't had a difficult performance conversation. They've never calibrated their instincts against a broader management team. What they need is scaffolding: written examples of strong versus weak feedback, role-plays of the difficult conversation, explicit coaching on how to deliver a below-expectations rating without destroying the relationship.

**Mid-level and experienced managers** need tools and reflection. They've run reviews before — sometimes good ones, sometimes bad ones. What they need is calibration on calibration: understanding where their tendencies run (too generous, too harsh, too influenced by recent performance), tools to correct for those tendencies, and a feedback loop that helps them understand how their people experienced the process.

In both cases, the investment in manager preparation pays off directly in the quality of the cycle. A well-prepared manager writes better assessments, runs better conversations, and delivers better outcomes for their team. The HR time spent on manager prep before a review cycle is almost always the highest-ROI activity in the whole process.

## Performance to Compensation: The Mechanics

The link between performance ratings and compensation changes is where most programs lose credibility. If the connection is opaque — ratings go in, merit increases come out, and no one can explain the math — people learn to distrust both. If the connection is too mechanical — a lookup table that turns "exceeds" into exactly 8% — it stops feeling evaluative and starts feeling like a game.

The approach I use threads this needle by making the mechanics transparent while preserving meaningful judgment:

- **Start from band position.** Where someone sits relative to their band midpoint is the first input. A person at 85% of midpoint has more room to grow toward market than someone at 110%. Adjustments account for this.
- **Apply performance differentiation.** Strong performance justifies movement toward or above midpoint. Developing performance may mean a smaller adjustment that still keeps someone on a path to market.
- **Publish the matrix.** An increase matrix — showing the range of increases for each performance rating at each band position — makes the process legible to managers and employees. It doesn't eliminate judgment, but it frames it.
- **Document exceptions.** When an increase falls outside the matrix (a retention situation, a correction for a promotion that happened mid-year, a role change), document the rationale. Undocumented exceptions are where inequity quietly accumulates.

## A Well-Designed Performance Cycle

<Steps>
  <Step title="Goal Setting and Role Clarity">
    At the start of the review period, every employee has documented goals tied to team and company priorities. Managers have a clear expectation of what "good" looks like at each level. The rubric is published and accessible.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Mid-Cycle Check-In">
    A structured mid-cycle conversation between manager and employee covers: progress against goals, feedback on strengths and development areas, and any adjustments to priorities. This creates an evidence checkpoint and prevents recency bias from dominating the final review.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Self-Assessment and Peer Feedback Collection">
    Four to six weeks before the review deadline, employees submit self-assessments covering impact, growth, and goals. Peer feedback is collected from a set of collaborators the manager approves. Both inputs feed the manager's written assessment.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Manager Assessment Drafting">
    Managers draft written assessments and preliminary ratings using the shared rubric. HR reviews a sample for calibration and level consistency before the calibration session. Coaching is provided where assessments are vague or misaligned.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Calibration Sessions">
    Cross-manager calibration sessions are run by HR with prepared managers. Ratings are discussed, inconsistencies surfaced and resolved, and final ratings agreed. The output is a calibrated distribution that reflects the actual performance of the population.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Compensation Planning">
    Calibrated ratings feed into the merit increase process. HR runs the increase matrix against current comp positioning and applies the budget. Exceptions are documented. Final increases are reviewed by leadership before communication.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Delivery and Development Planning">
    Managers deliver ratings and feedback in 1:1 conversations, supported by the written assessment. Every employee, regardless of rating, leaves with a clear understanding of their strengths, their development areas, and what growth looks like from here.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tip>
  **What I'd audit first at Mintlify**

  Before redesigning anything, I'd want to understand what the current review process actually produces. My first audit would focus on three things: rating distribution (is it compressed? is it consistent across teams?), the quality of written feedback (is it specific and evidenced, or generic and impressionistic?), and the connection to comp (do people understand how their rating translated into their increase?). Those three data points tell you almost everything about where the cycle is working and where it's breaking down — and they tell you which problems are design problems versus execution problems.
</Tip>
