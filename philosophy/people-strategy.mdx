The best people strategy is invisible to most people inside the company. They don't feel the strategy — they just feel like they can do their best work. That's the goal. People strategy, done right, is about creating the conditions: the clarity, safety, trust, and infrastructure that let talented people focus on building. At a 65-person company like Mintlify, that work is both urgent and delicate — you're past the point where founders can hold all the context, and you haven't yet built the systems that scale without them. That gap is where a deliberate people strategy either saves you or costs you.

My approach to people strategy starts with listening. Not periodic pulse surveys sent quarterly and never acted on, but a continuous architecture of signal collection that surfaces what's actually happening before it becomes a retention problem or a cultural rupture. From there, it moves to retention — understanding what keeps great people, what puts them at risk, and how to intervene early. And it ends with accountability: turning all of that signal into a clear, honest people-health read that leadership can act on every week.

## Listening Infrastructure

The goal of listening infrastructure isn't to accumulate data — it's to surface the signal you need to act before it's too late. At 65 people, the informal channels that worked at 20 are already breaking down. At 150, they'll be gone entirely. What replaces them has to be intentional.

A well-designed listening system has three layers:

**Continuous ambient signal.** This is the lightweight, always-on layer: manager 1:1 notes tracked in a shared template, optional monthly mood checks, and a clear norm that HR is a safe first stop for employees navigating hard things. The goal isn't surveillance — it's making sure there's a structured way for people to surface concerns before they become decisions.

**Periodic structured touchpoints.** Every six months, I run stay conversations with a representative sample of the team — not just top performers, but people at every level. These aren't performance conversations. They're about: What's energizing you? What's getting in your way? What would make you consider leaving? The answers are anonymized and synthesized into a brief for leadership. Done consistently, they become the most reliable retention signal you have.

**Proactive skip-levels.** At 65 people, a People leader should be in the flow of the organization. Regular skip-levels with ICs and team leads — not to go around managers, but to triangulate and catch what doesn't surface in structured channels — are essential. The goal is to understand the texture of the organization: where trust is strong, where it's thin, where something is quietly going wrong.

### Turning Signal into Insight

Listening infrastructure only creates value when it feeds into decisions. My practice is to synthesize all incoming signal — 1:1 notes, stay conversations, exit feedback, skip-level themes, tenure and attrition data — into a **weekly people-health brief** for the exec team. This brief covers:

- Active flight risks (named, with context, with proposed intervention)
- Open people issues in progress
- Engagement and sentiment trend (directional, not just a number)
- Headcount health: open roles, offer status, recent starts
- One "watch" item — a pattern or individual situation worth monitoring

The brief is honest. It doesn't smooth over problems. Leadership needs to know what's actually happening, and they need to hear it consistently enough that it becomes a habit, not a fire drill.

## Retention: Catching Risk Before Departure

Most retention interventions happen too late. By the time someone gives notice, they've often been mentally gone for weeks or months. The goal of a retention architecture is to catch the signal upstream — not at the exit interview.

### Leading Indicators to Watch

These are the signals I track as early warnings that someone is at flight risk:

- **Tenure at level** — People who've been in the same role for 18+ months without a clear path forward are at elevated risk, especially in high-growth companies where peers are leveling up.
- **Manager relationship quality** — Changes in the quality of a manager relationship are the single most predictive departure signal. A great employee who stops proactively sharing context with their manager is telling you something.
- **Engagement pattern shifts** — Reduced participation in non-required meetings, shorter 1:1s, declining initiative on cross-functional work. These are behavioral, not attitudinal, and they show up before someone says anything.
- **Compensation positioning** — People who discover they're at the bottom of their band, or who receive competitive offers they don't share with their manager, are running a quiet comparison. Pay equity and transparency matter here.
- **Life circumstances** — Partner relocation, life transitions, growing family. These don't cause departures but they remove the friction that keeps people from considering it.

### Intervention Playbooks

When I flag a flight risk, I don't just report it to leadership and wait. I build a playbook for the manager — sometimes running it with them directly — that includes:

1. An honest stay conversation within two weeks
2. A clear articulation of the growth path forward, even if it's not perfectly defined
3. A compensation review if positioning is a factor
4. A check on workload and team health

The key insight here is that most departures aren't inevitable. People rarely leave because of one thing. They leave because of accumulated friction — and when you catch that friction early, you often have real options.

<Note>
**What's different at 65 people vs. 500 people**

At 500 people, informal culture is mostly gone. You've built systems because you had to. At 65 people, informal culture is still alive — but it's fragile. Founders and early employees still carry enormous context in their heads. The CEO still knows everyone's name, their career goals, what frustrates them.

The risk is that you mistake that informal intimacy for infrastructure. It isn't. When a company like Mintlify is about to grow from 65 to 150+ people, the informal channels don't scale — they collapse. The CEO can't have a 1:1 intuition about 150 people. The leadership team can't hold the context they currently hold.

What has to replace it is structured, deliberately built, and maintained consistently. That means formalized listening systems, documented manager expectations, a people-health brief that doesn't rely on someone walking down the hall. The window to build this infrastructure is right now — before the growth wave, not after it.
</Note>

## People Health as an Executive Practice

One of the most important things a People leader can do at Mintlify's stage is make people health a genuine exec team priority — not just an HR update. That means building the habit of a regular people-health read, with enough consistency and rigor that the exec team starts treating it like they treat a financial dashboard.

Mintlify just raised its Series B. It's about to grow. The next 18 months will test the culture in ways the team hasn't experienced before. The people who are here now are the cultural foundation — their retention, their engagement, their ability to grow into more senior roles as the company scales — that's the whole game. Getting the listening infrastructure right now, before the growth, is what makes the difference between a company that scales its culture and one that loses it.
